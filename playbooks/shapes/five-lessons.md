# Shape: Five Lessons (`five-lessons`)

> last_researched: 2026-06-11 (as of June 2026). Length: long. Identity: any
> The worked example is invented. See `../README.md`.

## What one idea becomes

A real stretch of experience (years in a trade, a number of customers, a project from start to
finish) becomes **five lessons, each with the moment that taught it**. The experience earns the
right to be heard. The moments make the lessons believable. The advice makes them useful.

Choose it when the contract holds the experience as a real figure and a real moment behind each
lesson. A lesson with no moment behind it is an opinion. If only three lessons have moments, write three.

## The structure, beat by beat

1. **Opening (1 or 2 lines).** Earn the right: what was done, for how long, how many. Real
   figures from the contract, stated plainly. No adjectives.
2. **The promise (1 or 2 lines).** Five lessons, and who they are useful to. If they apply beyond
   your own trade, say so. That widens the readership without losing the subject.
3. **Each lesson, four parts:**
   - **The lesson,** as one plain, numbered sentence.
   - **What I believed before** (1 line).
   - **The moment that changed it** (2 or 3 lines). A specific day, a specific thing that happened.
   - **What to do with it** (1 line), as an instruction the reader can use.
4. **Close (2 lines).** The one lesson you would keep if you could keep only one. Then a
   question that invites the reader's own version.

**Ordering.** Start with the lesson that is easiest to agree with. End with the one that says
most about what the brand values. Put the most surprising one second, where many readers are
deciding whether to go on.

**Each lesson stands alone.** A reader who only reads lesson 3 should still get something. That
is also what lets the post be cut into a carousel.

## Length

| Platform | Length |
|---|---|
| LinkedIn | 1,500 to 2,500 characters. This shape uses the room |
| Facebook | 800 to 1,200, from a person. Keep the lesson, the moment in one line, and the instruction |
| Instagram | Carousel of 7 slides (cover, five lessons, the one to keep) or a note-style card, with a caption of 300 to 600 |
| X | A thread of 6 or 7 posts, one lesson each |

## Which platforms it suits, and why

- **LinkedIn:** its home. Experience plus specifics is what earns trust and reading time there,
  and numbered lessons get saved.
- **Facebook:** works from a person's profile, where it reads as someone looking back.
- **Instagram:** one lesson per slide. The note-style card suits the looking-back tone.

## Voice: a person or a company

- **A person (says "I").** The natural voice for this shape. "I used to hide my bad loaves."
  Admitting a mistake is what makes the lesson land.
- **A company (says "we").** "Four years of Saturday classes taught us five things." Use "we
  believed" and "we changed". It must still contain real moments. A company's lessons with no
  moments read like a values poster.

## The rule

**Fill it only from the contract you were given. Never invent a fact, number, quote or
customer.** The opening figure must be real and checkable. The moments must have happened. If a
moment involves a customer, keep them unidentifiable unless the contract includes their permission.

## Worked example (Maple & Main Bakery, invented)

Platform: LinkedIn. Identity: Ava Lindqvist, head baker (a person). Visual: a LinkedIn PDF, one lesson per page.

```text
I have taught a little over 1,400 people to bake their first loaf.

Four years of Saturday mornings, eight beginners at a time.
Here are five things they taught me about teaching. They apply well beyond bread.

1. People do not need more information. They need one less thing to worry about.

I used to open class with twenty minutes on flour types.
Then I watched a room of adults stop listening at minute three.
Now we have our hands in dough inside five minutes.
Cut your first explanation in half, then start.

2. The question they ask is rarely the question they have.

I believed "how long do I knead?" was about kneading.
It usually means "how will I know if I am doing it wrong?".
So I stopped giving times and started showing what ready dough feels like.
Answer the worry, not the words.

3. Nobody remembers step seven.

My first handout had fourteen steps.
People followed it perfectly in class and failed at home.
Now it has four steps, and a photo of each.
If it does not fit on a fridge door, it is too long.

4. A small failure in the room is worth more than a perfect demo.

I used to hide my own bad loaves.
One Saturday my demo loaf stuck to the tin in front of everyone.
That class asked better questions than any before it.
Let people see you fix something.

5. They came for the bread. They come back for the table.

I assumed a returning customer wanted harder recipes.
When I asked, most said they wanted another morning with flour on their hands and no phone.
So the second class is slower, not harder.
Ask people why they returned before you build the next thing.

If I could keep only one, it would be the second.

What is a question your customers ask that is really about something else?
```

What to notice: the opening figure is plain and checkable. Every lesson has a "before", a moment
and an instruction. The lessons are about teaching, so they travel beyond baking while staying
inside what Ava really knows. The close picks one and hands the question to the reader.
