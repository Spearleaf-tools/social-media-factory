# Shape: Three Tips (`three-tips`)

> last_researched: 2026-06-11 (as of June 2026). Length: long. Identity: any
> The worked example is invented. See `../README.md`.

## What one idea becomes

One subject the reader wants to get better at becomes **three things they can do this week**.
Each tip carries its reason and one concrete detail, so the reader could act on it without
asking a follow-up question. This is the deepest of the teaching shapes and the most saved.

Choose it when the contract holds three real, distinct actions. If there are only two good
ones, write two. A padded third tip is the one readers remember.

## The structure, beat by beat

1. **Opening (1 or 2 lines).** Who this is for and what they will be able to do. Fit the
   platform's visible length. Pick a pattern from `../hooks.md`.
2. **The stake (2 or 3 lines).** What usually goes wrong, and the good news that it comes down to
   a few small things.
3. **Tip 1.**
   - The action, as an instruction, in one line.
   - Why it works, in 1 or 2 lines.
   - What it looks like in practice: one concrete detail from the contract.
4. **Tip 2.** The same three parts. Add the common slip if there is one.
5. **Tip 3.** The same. Make this the one most people skip, and say so.
6. **Close (1 or 2 lines).** Which tip to start with. Then one ask: a save prompt that names the
   moment they will need this.

**Ordering.** Put the tips in the order the reader will meet them. Where there is no natural
order, go from easiest to the one people skip.

**Parallel form.** All three tips start the same way (a number, then an instruction). The reader
should be able to find tip 2 at a glance.

## Length

| Platform | Length |
|---|---|
| LinkedIn | 1,000 to 1,800 characters |
| Facebook | 500 to 900. Keep the three instructions and one line of reason each |
| Instagram | Carousel of 6 slides (cover, stake, three tips, recap) plus a caption of 300 to 600 |
| X | A thread of 5 posts: opening, three tips, recap. Each within 280 |

## Which platforms it suits, and why

- **LinkedIn:** long reading time and saves are what LinkedIn rewards, and a numbered method earns both.
- **Instagram:** three tips are three slides. The recap slide is the one that gets saved.
- **Facebook:** works shortened, from a person, with a question at the end.
- **X:** only as a thread, and only if each tip can stand alone.

## Voice: a person or a company

- **A person (says "I").** "Three things I tell every beginner." The tips are things you do or
  teach. A line about how you learned one is welcome, if it is in the contract.
- **A company (says "we" and "you").** "Three things to check before your first loaf." Instructions
  address "you". Anything about the business is "we": "In our Saturday class we..."

Either way, instructions are plain imperatives: "Wet your hands." Not "It can be helpful to consider wetting your hands."

## The rule

**Fill it only from the contract you were given. Never invent a fact, number, quote or
customer.** If the contract has no concrete detail for a tip, the tip stays general or goes. A
made-up statistic is worse than none.

## Worked example (Maple & Main Bakery, invented)

Platform: LinkedIn. Identity: Ava Lindqvist, head baker (a person). Visual: a note-style card listing the three tips.

```text
Three things I tell every beginner before their first sourdough loaf.

Most first loaves come out flat.
It is almost never the recipe.
It is one of three small things, and all three are easy to fix.

1. Use your starter while it is rising, not after it has peaked.

A starter that has already fallen has spent its strength.
Most beginners wait until the jar looks its fullest. By then it is tiring.
Drop a spoonful in water. If it floats, it is ready.
In our Saturday class, this one check fixes about half the flat loaves.

2. Wet your hands instead of adding flour.

Sticky dough makes people reach for more flour.
More flour gives a tight, dry crumb.
The slip I see most is a little extra flour at every fold. It adds up.
Wet hands stop the sticking and leave the dough alone.

3. Let the loaf cool for a full hour.

This is the one nearly everybody skips.
The inside is still cooking when the loaf leaves the oven.
Cut it early and the crumb turns gummy, however well you baked it.

If you change only one thing this weekend, make it the float test.

Save this for Friday night, when you feed your starter.
```

What to notice: the opening names the reader. Every tip has a reason and a detail. The third
tip is flagged as the skipped one. The close picks a starting point, and the ask names a moment.
