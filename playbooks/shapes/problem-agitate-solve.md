# Shape: Problem, Cost, Way Out (`problem-agitate-solve`)

> last_researched: 2026-06-11 (as of June 2026). Length: medium. Identity: any
> The worked example is invented. See `../README.md`.

## What one idea becomes

One problem the reader already feels becomes **a short piece of persuasion**: the problem as they
live it, what it costs them if nothing changes, and a real first step out. It is the classic
three-part persuasion structure, used here to move someone toward a small action, not to frighten them.

Choose it when the contract holds a problem in the customer's own words (the brand's audience
file is the source) and a way out that the brand can actually explain or offer.

## The structure, beat by beat

1. **The problem, as a scene (2 or 3 lines).** Show the moment, in the reader's words. "You buy a
   good loaf on Saturday and by Monday it is hard" is a scene. "Bread storage issues" is a label.
2. **The cost (2 or 3 lines).** What this costs if nothing changes: money, time, comfort, a small
   daily irritation. Keep it true and in proportion.
3. **The turn (1 line).** The problem is fixable, or the cause is not the one they think.
4. **The way out (3 to 5 lines).** What to do, first step first. Give the whole method in the
   post. A numbered list works well.
5. **Proof (1 or 2 lines, optional).** A result or detail from the contract. Skip the beat if there is none.
6. **The ask (1 line).** One next step. Either a real offer from the brand's offers table, or a
   question, a save or a send. Not two of them.

**On the cost beat.** This is the beat that can go wrong. Describe, do not predict. Never invent
a consequence, never enlarge a fear, and on a regulated brand (health, finance, law) keep to what
the brand has approved. If the honest cost is small, say it is small. Small, daily and true
persuades better than large and doubtful.

## Length

| Platform | Length |
|---|---|
| Facebook | 500 to 800 characters |
| LinkedIn | 900 to 1,400 |
| Instagram | Carousel of 6 or 7 slides (scene, cost, turn, the steps, ask), caption of 300 to 500 |
| X | A thread of 4 or 5 posts, only when the steps are worth a post each |

## Which platforms it suits, and why

- **Facebook:** a recognisable everyday problem draws long comments from people who have it, and
  comment length and relevance are ranking signals there.
- **LinkedIn:** the scene earns the tap on "see more", and the method earns the save.
- **Instagram:** works as a carousel. The slide with the steps is the one that gets saved.

## Voice: a person or a company

- **A person (says "I").** The scene can be your own: "I used to throw away half of every loaf."
  The way out is what you do.
- **A company (says "we" and "you").** The scene is the reader's: "You buy a good loaf..." The way
  out is "here is what we do" or "here is what to do".

Both: the problem is described with sympathy. The reader is never the villain of their own problem.

## The rule

**Fill it only from the contract you were given. Never invent a fact, number, quote or
customer.** That includes the cost beat: no invented statistics about how much the problem costs
"the average person". If the brand has no figure, describe the cost in words.

## Worked example (Maple & Main Bakery, invented)

Platform: Facebook. Identity: Maple & Main (a company). Visual: a photo of a loaf cut side down on a board.

```text
You buy a good loaf on Saturday. By Monday evening it is a doorstop.

So half of it goes in the bin.
Or you give up and go back to sliced bread in a bag.
Either way you paid for a whole loaf and ate half.

The bread is not the problem. The fridge is.

Bread goes stale fastest at fridge temperature.
Here is what we do with our own loaves at home.

1. Day one: keep it cut side down on a board. No bag.
2. Day two: move it into a cloth bag or a bread bin.
3. Slice and freeze on day one whatever you will not eat by day three. We will slice it at the counter if you ask.
4. Toast it straight from the freezer. It comes back better than you expect.

Kept this way, one of our sourdough loaves is good for four to five days.

How do you keep yours? We are always looking for a better trick.
```

What to notice: the scene is one the reader has lived. The cost is small and true. The turn
moves the blame off the reader. The full method is in the post. The offer (slicing at the
counter) sits inside a step, so the only ask is the question.
