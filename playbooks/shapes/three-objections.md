# Shape: Three Objections, Answered (`three-objections`)

> last_researched: 2026-06-11 (as of June 2026). Length: long. Identity: any
> The worked example is invented. See `../README.md`.

## What one idea becomes

One thing the reader wants and still has not done becomes **the three reasons they give, each
one taken seriously and then answered**. It is the shape for the gap between wanting and acting.
Done well, the reader feels understood, not argued with.

Choose it when the contract holds real objections in customers' own words. The brand's audience
file has a section called "Objections we hear". If that section is empty, do not guess. Choose another shape.

## The structure, beat by beat

1. **Opening (1 or 2 lines).** The thing people want, and the fact that many never start. No blame.
2. **Bridge (1 or 2 lines).** You have asked why, and you hear the same three reasons.
3. **Each objection, four parts:**
   - **The objection, quoted** in the customer's words. One line, in quote marks.
   - **What is right about it** (1 line). Always concede something true. This is the line that
     keeps the reader reading.
   - **The fact that changes the picture** (1 to 3 lines), from the contract: a number, a detail
     of how the brand actually works, an example.
   - **The smallest next step** (1 line). Something they could do today, for free.
4. **Close (2 or 3 lines).** What the three have in common, then one ask.

**Ordering.** Go from the surface reason to the real one. The practical objections (time, money,
kit) come first. The one people rarely say aloud (fear of looking foolish, fear it will not work
for them) comes last, and you can say that it is the real one.

**Tone.** You are dismantling the objection, never the person. If a reader finishes feeling
lectured, the post has failed, however good the arguments.

## Length

| Platform | Length |
|---|---|
| LinkedIn | 1,000 to 2,000 characters |
| Instagram | Carousel of 8 slides (cover, bridge, two slides per objection or one each plus a recap), caption of 300 to 500 |
| Facebook | 700 to 1,000, with each objection cut to the quote, the fact and the step |
| X | Not recommended. If used, a thread of 5 posts |

## Which platforms it suits, and why

- **LinkedIn:** the long form suits it, and quoted objections invite comments from people who
  recognise their own.
- **Instagram:** one objection per slide, the quote in large type. The recap slide gets saved,
  and the whole thing gets sent to the friend who keeps putting it off.
- **Facebook:** works shortened, ending on a question about which objection was theirs.

## Voice: a person or a company

- **A person (says "I").** "When I ask, I hear the same three reasons." You may admit that you
  held one of these objections yourself, if the contract says so.
- **A company (says "we" and "you").** "When we ask people at the counter..." The concession line
  matters even more here. A company that concedes nothing sounds like a sales page.

## The rule

**Fill it only from the contract you were given. Never invent a fact, number, quote or
customer.** The objections must be ones the brand has really heard. The quotes are typical
wording, not attributed to a named customer unless the contract includes that customer and their permission.

## Worked example (Maple & Main Bakery, invented)

Platform: LinkedIn. Identity: Ava Lindqvist, head baker (a person). Visual: a LinkedIn PDF, one objection per page.

```text
Most people who tell me they want to bake bread never book a class.

I used to think it was the price. It is not.
When I ask, I hear the same three reasons.

"I do not have the time."

You are right that a loaf takes 14 hours.
But your hands are busy for about 20 minutes of that.
The dough does the rest while you sleep or work.
Try it once: mix after dinner tonight, and bake tomorrow morning.

"I do not have the kit."

It is true that the photos online are full of special baskets and cast iron pots.
In our beginners' class we use a mixing bowl, a tea towel and a roasting tin with a lid.
Most kitchens already have all three.
Look in your cupboard before you buy anything.

"I will be the worst one there."

This is the real one, and hardly anybody says it out loud.
Every Saturday class has eight people, and most have never made a loaf.
Every one of them leaves with bread they are proud of.
The worst loaf I have seen in class was still eaten by lunchtime.

None of the three is really about bread.
They are all about starting.

Which one was yours, before you baked your first loaf?
```

What to notice: each objection is in the customer's words. Each answer opens by agreeing with
something. The facts are the brand's own (class size, the kit list, the timings). The deepest
objection comes last and is named as the real one. There is one ask.
