# LinkedIn rulebook

> last_researched: 2026-06-11 (as of June 2026). Confidence: medium
> Basis: LinkedIn publishes almost nothing about how it ranks posts. What follows comes from
> several large third-party studies (hundreds of thousands to over a million posts each) that
> agree with each other. The directions are dependable. The exact multipliers are estimates.
> Every example is invented. See `README.md`.

## 1. How reach works there now

**A post goes through three stages.**

1. **The check (first hour).** The post is scanned for spam and engagement bait, and its
   subject is worked out from the text. It is shown to a small slice of your network, a few percent.
2. **The test (roughly the first 60 to 90 minutes).** The quality of the early response decides
   whether the post travels beyond your own connections. Quality means real comments, time spent
   reading and saves. A handful of thoughtful comments beats a rush of quick reactions.
3. **The long tail (two to three days, sometimes weeks).** A post that holds attention keeps
   being shown. Posts no longer die after a day. Saved posts come back round.

**What counts, strongest first.**

| Signal | What it means | How you earn it |
|---|---|---|
| Reading time | People stopped and stayed | Brief lines, a single thought in each, something to look at |
| Saves | "I will need this again" | A list, a checklist, a process, a recap slide |
| Comments with substance | Longer comments and replies to replies count most | One real question. Then answer everyone |
| Reshares | Passed on | A point that makes the sharer look informed |
| Reactions | Weakest | Do not write for them |

**Subject beats connections.** LinkedIn reads the post, decides what it is about, and shows it
to people who have shown interest in that subject, whether or not they know you. An account that
stays on a few subjects is matched well. An account that wanders teaches the system the wrong
audience, and later posts go to the wrong people.

**People out-reach pages, by a lot.** A company page post reaches a very small share of the
page's followers (studies put it under two in a hundred). The same words from a person's profile
reach many times more. If the brand has a person who can post, that profile is the main channel.
The page is where people check that the business is real.

**Reach is down for everyone.** Studies report that reach across the platform fell by a third to
a half during 2025. Do not judge a post by impressions. Judge it by saves, comments from the right
people, profile visits and messages.

## 2. First lines that earn the click

**The limit.** About 140 characters show on a phone before "see more" (about 210 on a desktop).
Most reading happens on a phone, and many readers never tap through. For them the opening
is all they will read. A line break ends the visible part early, so the opening is one tight paragraph.

**What a strong opening has.**

- Something specific: a number, a time span, a named outcome.
- A gap: the reader wants the next line.
- A signal of who it is for.

**Patterns that work here** (templates and more examples in `hooks.md`):

| Pattern | Invented example |
|---|---|
| The result first | "Our Saturday class sold out in 9 days with no adverts. One change to the booking page did it." |
| The common belief, corrected | "Most runners I see think rest fixes a sore knee. For the runners I treat, rest is usually what keeps it coming back." |
| "I looked at N of these" | "I read 40 feedback cards from our baking classes last month. 31 mentioned the same five minutes." |
| What changed | "Flour went up again in May. Here is what that does to the price of one loaf, line by line." |
| The list worth keeping | "The 7 checks I run before I let a runner go back to hills:" |
| The honest admission | "I told a customer not to book our class last week. Here is why." |

**Openings that fail:** scene-setting that could begin any post by anyone, a question with an
obvious answer, a greeting, your job title, and anything that sounds like a motivational poster.
They also attract the wrong readers, which costs you later (see section 6).

## 3. Post skeletons

### A. The Shown Result (text plus one image)
*Use it when you have something real to show: a photo, a chart, a before and after, a page from the order book.*

```
[OPENING, 140 characters or fewer: the result or finding, and who it is for]

[CONTEXT: set the scene in a line or two]

[WALK THROUGH THE IMAGE, 3 to 6 lines of about 12 words. Each line makes a single point.
 "Look at the third row." "That dip is the week we changed the price."]

[THE TAKEAWAY, 2 or 3 lines: the principle the reader can use. This earns the save.]

[CLOSE: one real question, or one line to remember.]
```
Attach one or two real images. Tall images read best on a phone. Aim for 900 to 1,500 characters.

### B. The Keepable Document (PDF carousel)
*Use it when the idea is a list, a checklist or a process someone will want again.*

```
Slide 1      Title and who it is for ("7 checks before you go back to hill running")
Slide 2      The reason to care today, shown with a single figure or picture
Slides 3-8   A single point each. Keep to 6 to 8 lines, in type of 24pt or larger.
             Each slide pairs the point with its evidence or example.
Slide 9      Recap: every point on one slide. This is the slide people screenshot.
Slide 10     Quiet close: name, what you do, and when to use this. Leave links off it.
Caption      2 to 4 lines: the opening, then what is inside.
```
Study after study puts document posts first on LinkedIn, because every swipe counts as
attention. Six to ten slides is the dependable range.

### C. The Short Lesson (text only)
*Use it when the point is an opinion or a lesson and the writing can carry it without a picture.*

```
[OPENING: the lesson or the belief you are correcting, in one line]

[THE MOMENT, 2 to 4 lines: when you learned it. A specific day, a specific customer type, what happened.]

[THE TURN, 1 line: what you do differently now]

[WHY, 2 or 3 lines: the reason it works]

[CLOSE: a question for people who have faced the same thing]
```
Keep paragraphs to three lines or fewer. White space is part of the writing here.

### D. The Quick Test (short vertical video, or a text post that documents a test)
*Use it when you can try something in front of the reader and show what happens.*

```
[OPENING: "I tried X. Watch what happens." or the written equivalent]
[SETUP: one line that puts the test as something your reader wonders about]
[THE TEST: the recording, or the steps and what you saw at each]
[WHY, 2 or 3 lines: what decided the outcome]
[TAKEAWAY: the one thing to do about it]
```
Video: vertical, under 60 seconds, captions burned in or uploaded. Most LinkedIn video plays
silently, and captions lift watch time noticeably.

## 4. Length, links, hashtags and calls to action

**Length.** The cap is 3,000 characters. In a study of several hundred thousand posts, 1,300 to
2,500 characters had the highest median engagement, and posts under 400 the lowest. Working
range: 900 to 2,500. Never pad to reach a number. The 140 character opening matters more than
the total.

**Links.** This changed, and older advice is wrong.

- A link in the post body costs a large share of reach. Studies range from a fifth to a half.
- A link in the first comment used to be the workaround. LinkedIn now spots posts written mainly
  to steer people toward a link in the comments, and trims their reach too, by roughly a tenth.
- Default: no link on a post meant to travel. Put the substance in the post.
- When the link is the point (an event, a sign-up), accept the cost. The first comment still
  beats the body.

**Hashtags.** Zero to three, specific, or none. LinkedIn removed hashtag following, and in recent
datasets posts without hashtags did slightly better. Spend no effort here. Write the
subject into your sentences so the classifier reads it. Many brands switch hashtags off on
LinkedIn entirely. The brand's style setting wins.

**Calls to action.** One per post.

| Allowed | Not allowed |
|---|---|
| A specific question only your reader can answer from experience ("Runners: what finally made you book an assessment?") | "Comment YES if you agree" |
| A save prompt that names the moment ("Save this for the week before your first class") | "Tag someone who needs this" |
| A plain statement of what you do, in an occasional offer post | The same sales line on every post |

Engagement bait is a recognised pattern and the post is held back. Keep sales asks in their own
post, about one in ten, and accept that it will reach fewer people.

**Rhythm.** Three to five posts a week. Not more than one a day. Reply to comments within about
15 minutes during the first hour or so. Replies to replies extend reach more than anything else you
can do after posting.

## 5. Formats that work

1. **Document (PDF) carousel.** The save engine. A weekly checklist, process or breakdown.
2. **Image post.** A real photo, chart or screenshot plus text. Cheapest to make, easy to believe.
3. **Native vertical video, under 60 seconds, captioned.** Rising. Good home for clips cut from longer recordings.
4. **Text only.** Opinion and story, when the writing is good.
5. **Polls.** They can reach far, but they are in decline and look cheap. Rarely, and only with a question worth asking.

## 6. What to avoid

1. **Reach from the wrong people.** A broad, emotional, off-subject post can travel far and bring
   readers who will never be customers. LinkedIn then shows your next posts to more people like them.
   Measure a post by whether possible customers kept it, not by how many strangers tapped like.
2. **Engagement bait and engagement groups.** Detected and suppressed.
3. **A pitch on the end of every post.** Readers learn to skip you.
4. **Copy that sounds machine-written.** Formula openings, three-part lists everywhere, empty
   adjectives. Specific detail from the brand's own files is the cure.
5. **Claims with nothing behind them.** "Our customers love us" slides past unread. The photo,
   the number and the page from the order book are what stop the scroll. If a draft has nothing the reader can see
   or check, it is not ready.
6. **Links in the body, stuffed hashtags, more than one post a day, dense jargon.**
7. **Judging by impressions.** Track how often posts are saved and how many messages arrive, one quarter against the last.

## 7. Applying this to your brand

The rulebook says how. Your brand files say what. Before writing, read:

| You need | Read |
|---|---|
| Who is speaking, and whether it is "I" or "we" | `Brand/brand-voice.md`, and the identities in `brand-master.json` |
| How the brand really sounds | `Brand/voice-samples.md`. Echo it. Do not smooth it |
| Who the reader is, and the questions and objections they really have | `Brand/audience.md` |
| What the brand talks about | `Brand/pillars-and-offers.md`. Stay inside the pillars. That is what builds subject authority here |
| What may be offered, and the next step | The offers table in `Brand/pillars-and-offers.md` |
| What can be shown or stated | The facts table in `Brand/business-profile.md`, and the proof in `Inputs/` and `Studio/` |

Decisions to make per brand:

- **Person or page?** If the brand has a person identity, LinkedIn posts go from that profile first.
- **Which readers?** Name them in the opening, using the words in `Brand/audience.md`.
- **Which proof?** Every Shown Result needs a real item from the proof library. No item, choose skeleton C.
- **Regulated brand?** (Health, finance, law.) Write outcomes as what can happen, never as a promise.
  Use only claims the brand has approved. The checker enforces the brand's own term lists.
