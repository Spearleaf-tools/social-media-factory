# Why things spread

> last_researched: 2026-06-11 (as of June 2026)
> The platform facts referred to here (which actions each feed rewards) are in the platform
> rulebooks. The mechanisms themselves are about people, and change far more slowly.
> Every example is invented. See `README.md`.

A post does not spread because it is good. It spreads because a particular person had a
particular reason to **keep** it, **send** it or **answer** it. This file lists those reasons,
so that the planner can choose one on purpose for every post.

## The three actions worth designing for

| Action | What the person is thinking | What the platforms do with it |
|---|---|---|
| **Save** (save, bookmark) | "I will need this again" | Strong signal on LinkedIn, Instagram, X and short video. The post also comes back round later |
| **Send** (share, message, repost) | "This is for you" or "this says something about me" | The strongest signal for reaching new people on Instagram, Facebook and short video |
| **Answer** (a real comment or reply) | "I have something to add" | Longer, on-subject comments count most everywhere |

A like is not on the list. It costs the reader nothing, so it tells the platform little.

**Pick one action per post.** A post built to be saved looks different from a post built to be
sent. Then pick the mechanism below that produces that action, and check the contract has what
the mechanism needs.

| If you want | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Saves | 1. Worth keeping, 7. A process shown, 8. A result you can check |
| Sends | 2. "This is so you", 3. A named feeling, 4. A surprising reversal, 5. Social currency |
| Answers | 6. A fight worth picking, 9. A question only they can answer, 10. Belonging |
| Any of the three, sooner | 11. Right now |

## 1. Worth keeping

**What it is.** A thing the reader will use again at a specific future moment: a checklist, a
timetable, a set of ratios, the steps in order.

**Why it works.** Saving is a promise to a future self. People save what would be annoying to
find again and what they expect to need at a moment they can picture.

**How to use it honestly.** Name the moment ("for Sunday's bake", "for the week before your
race"). Put the whole thing on one slide or in one block, so the saved item works alone. Make it
complete: a checklist with the hard step missing is an advert, not a tool.

**Failure mode.** "Save this!" on a post with nothing to keep. Also lists of obvious advice. If
the reader already knows every item, there is nothing to save.

*Example:* Maple & Main's one-slide timetable for a loaf that fits round a working day: when to feed, mix, fold, shape and bake.

## 2. "This is so you"

**What it is.** A post that describes a specific kind of person so exactly that the reader
thinks of someone by name.

**Why it works.** Sending it is a small act of friendship: "I saw this and thought of you."
The sender gets the credit for the find. The platform sees a send.

**How to use it honestly.** Describe a real, recognisable situation from `Brand/audience.md`,
kindly. Aim the ask at the person: "Send this to the friend who keeps saying they will start
running in spring." One slide or one line should be written for the person receiving it, who
arrives with no context.

**Failure mode.** Mockery. If the person receiving it would feel laughed at, nobody sends it, or
worse, they do. And "tag a friend" is bait. The send has to be the reader's own idea.

*Example:* Stride Physio's "the five stages of pretending your ankle is fine", each one gentle and true.

## 3. A named feeling

**What it is.** Putting words to something the reader has felt and never said: the dread of the
first run back after an injury, the small shame of a collapsed loaf after a day's work.

**Why it works.** Being understood is rare and people pass it on. It also signals that the brand
knows the customer's life from the inside, which no claim of expertise can do.

**How to use it honestly.** Take the feeling from real conversations, reviews and questions in
the brand's files, in the customer's own words where possible. Name it, stay with it for a line
or two, then offer one small, real way forward. A person does this better than a company.

**Failure mode.** Inventing a feeling the audience does not have, or milking a real one to sell.
On a regulated brand, never deepen a fear in order to offer the cure.

*Example:* Ava Lindqvist on why her first ten loaves went in the bin, and what it felt like to cut the eleventh.

## 4. A surprising reversal

**What it is.** The reader expects one thing and learns the opposite, with proof: the quick loaf
that beats the slow one on a blind taste, the expensive shoe that changes nothing.

**Why it works.** Surprise is what makes something worth telling someone else. "Did you know"
is one of the oldest reasons to speak.

**How to use it honestly.** The reversal must be true, and the evidence must follow within a
line or two. State the common belief fairly, in the words people use. Say where the old belief is
still right. The most believable reversals are small and exact.

**Failure mode.** Contrarian for its own sake. A bold reversal with no evidence earns mutes and
"not interested", which now count against a post. It also fails if the belief you overturn is one
nobody actually holds.

*Example:* Maple & Main baking one dough after 4 hours and after 14, and cutting both open on camera.

## 5. Social currency

**What it is.** Something that makes the person sharing it look informed, helpful, funny or
ahead of the news.

**Why it works.** Every share is also a small statement about the sharer. People pass on what
makes them look like the person they want to be seen as.

**How to use it honestly.** Give them something real to be first with: a local change, a fresh
number from your own records, a clear explanation of something confusing. Make it easy to repeat
in one sentence. Keep the brand light on the shareable part. People share a useful chart. They do
not share an advert for you.

**Failure mode.** Borrowed facts with no source, or a statistic that turns out to be wrong. That
costs the sharer their credibility, and they will not share you twice.

*Example:* Stride Physio's one-slide explanation of why the first race of spring produces so many calf strains, with the clinic's own appointment counts by month.

## 6. A fight worth picking

**What it is.** Taking a clear side against a **practice**: a bad habit, a piece of tired advice,
a corner that the trade cuts.

**Why it works.** A position gives people something to agree with out loud, and gives the people
who disagree something to argue with. Both are real comments. It also tells the right customers
what you stand for.

**How to use it honestly.** Choose an enemy your customer already resents, not one that only
bothers people in your trade. Attack the practice, never a named person or business. Say what
you do instead, and show it. Check the brand's never-mention list and its "things we never say".

**Failure mode.** Picking on a competitor by name. Outrage as a habit. Attacking something the
brand itself sells or does. On most platforms, posts that provoke blocks and reports are now pushed down.

*Example:* Maple & Main against "fresh bread" that was baked elsewhere and warmed up, with a photo of their own 4am oven.

## 7. A process shown, not told

**What it is.** Letting people watch the work: the fold, the assessment, the mistake and the fix.

**Why it works.** Watching is proof. It also answers the question every customer has and rarely
asks: "what would actually happen if I came to you?" And a process with steps is naturally worth saving.

**How to use it honestly.** Show the real thing, including a small imperfection. Narrate in plain
words. Put the end result first where you can, so people know what they are waiting for. Get
permission for any customer who can be identified.

**Failure mode.** A staged process that does not match the real one. Too many steps. Footage with
no point, where the viewer never learns why they watched.

*Example:* Tomas Okafor filming the first three minutes of a running assessment, saying aloud what he is looking at and why.

## 8. A result you can check

**What it is.** A before and after, a number from your own records, a customer's words with
permission, a photo taken today.

**Why it works.** A claim is cheap. Evidence the reader can inspect is rare, so it gets kept as
a reference and passed on as proof.

**How to use it honestly.** Use only what is in the brand's proof library and facts table. Show
the date and the source where you can. Say what the result does not prove. One modest result,
fully shown, beats three large ones asserted.

**Failure mode.** Inflated or untraceable numbers. Results presented as typical when they are the
best ever. On regulated brands, testimonials and outcome claims may be restricted: follow the brand's compliance settings.

*Example:* Maple & Main's waste sheet for six weeks, photographed, with the week the whiteboard went up marked.

## 9. A question only they can answer

**What it is.** A question the reader can answer from their own life and from nowhere else.

**Why it works.** People like being asked about what they know. The answers are long and on the
subject, which is exactly what the feeds reward, and other readers enjoy them too.

**How to use it honestly.** Ask what you would really like to know. Make it specific and easy to
start answering. Answer it yourself first, briefly, to show the kind of reply you hope for. Then
reply to everyone who answers.

**Failure mode.** Questions with one-word answers, questions you do not care about, and any
"comment X" trade. Those are bait, and the platforms treat them as bait.

*Example:* "What was the run that made you think, I need to get this looked at?"

## 10. Belonging

**What it is.** A detail only insiders recognise: the smell of a starter that needs feeding, the
particular misery of mile 20.

**Why it works.** Recognition feels like membership. People respond to show they belong, and
share to show others they do.

**How to use it honestly.** Use true details from the brand's world. Keep the door open: a
newcomer should feel invited in, not shut out.

**Failure mode.** In-jokes that exclude the customer. A community the brand is not really part of.

*Example:* Maple & Main asking what people name their starters, and sharing the name of theirs.

## 11. Right now

**What it is.** A link to something happening this week: the season, a local event, a change in
the rules, a price rise.

**Why it works.** Timeliness gives people a reason to look today instead of later, and a reason
to tell others before it is old.

**How to use it honestly.** Add something the reader cannot get from the news itself: what it
means for them, from your position. In the Factory this is the `news` lane, which goes out the
same or next day instead of joining the drip.

**Failure mode.** Bolting the brand onto a story it has no real connection with. Commenting on
tragedy. Being late.

*Example:* Stride Physio in the week race entries open: the twelve weeks that matter if you are starting from zero.

## Using this when planning a post

1. **Name the action:** save, send or answer.
2. **Name the mechanism:** one from the list. Two at most.
3. **Check the contract has what it needs:** a real list, a real feeling from real customers, a real result, a real position.
4. **Choose the shape and format that carry it:** a checklist wants a carousel or a document. A reversal wants a side by side. A named feeling wants a person's voice and few words.
5. **Write the ask to match:** "save this for", "send this to", or the question.

## What all the failure modes have in common

Every mechanism here can be faked, and every fake works once. A faked result, a manufactured
feeling, a fight picked for attention, a question nobody wanted answered. The platforms now
detect most of it. Readers detect the rest. A brand that posts for years cannot afford either.
The honest version is slower and keeps working.
