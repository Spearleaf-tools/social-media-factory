# Short-form scripts: the craft

> last_researched: 2026-06-11 (as of June 2026). This playbook is craft, not platform rules. It
> states no limits, thresholds or ranking facts of its own. The few it leans on come from
> `short-form-video.md`, which carries that date. Every example is invented. See `README.md`.

This is how a script gets written in the Factory: what to decide before the first word, how the
opening is built, what every line needs, how a video ends, what happens after it, and how to get
better from your own numbers.

## 1. Decide the job before the subject

A short video can do one of three jobs. It cannot do all three at once, and most disappointing
videos are a good script written for the wrong job.

| Job | What it is for | A good subject | How it ends | Judge it by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Reach** | New people find out you exist | Something a stranger would send to a friend. Wide enough to travel, still plainly about your trade | Ask for a save, a send or a follow | Views, and sends |
| **Trust** | People already watching believe you a little more | One real question your customers ask, answered properly | A real question, or nothing at all | What people kept: saves and sends for every 1,000 views, and how much of the video they watched |
| **Leads** | The right people take one next step | A problem your offer solves, shown being solved | One next step the brand really offers | Next steps taken for every 1,000 views |

**How to choose.** Start from what the business needs this month, not from what might get views.

- Nobody has heard of you yet: more reach, but keep every subject inside your trade. A view from
  someone who will never buy teaches the platform to find more people like them. `short-form-video.md`
  section 6 explains why that hurts.
- People know you and still do not buy: trust. This is where most businesses that sell a service
  or a skill should spend most of their scripts.
- People trust you and there is a class, a booking or a product to fill: leads, sparingly. A feed
  that sells in every video stops being watched.

A sensible starting mix for a business with something to sell is mostly trust, some reach, and a
few leads. Treat that as a starting point and let your own audit move it.

**The job is written into the script.** Every script file carries `Job:` in its header. The job
decides which endings are allowed, and later it decides which number the script is judged by. A
reach script is never marked down for bringing no bookings, and a leads script is never praised
for views alone.

*Bakery.* "Why supermarket sourdough is often not sourdough" is reach: anyone who buys bread might
send it on. "Why your loaf comes out dense" is trust: it answers the question Ava hears at the
counter. "What you leave a Saturday class with" is leads: it ends by pointing at the class.

*Clinic.* "The stretch most runners do before a run does little for them" is reach. "Why heel pain
comes back after you rest it" is trust. "What happens in a first running assessment" is leads.

## 2. The opening: three layers, one promise

A viewer meets an opening three ways at once. They read a few words, they see a picture, and they
hear a sentence. Those are three chances to say the same thing, or three chances to confuse.

| Layer | In the script file | Its one job |
|---|---|---|
| The words on screen | `On screen:` | Name the subject so the right person knows it is for them |
| The first picture | `Show:` | Be the evidence for the words. Show the thing itself, already happening |
| The first spoken line | `Say:` | Make the same promise out loud, and leave one thing unanswered |

**Write the promise first.** Before any of the three layers, write one plain line: what does the
viewer get for staying? That line goes in the script as `Promise:`. It is never shown to anyone.
It is the yardstick the three layers are held against.

**Then build the layers in this order: words on screen, picture, spoken line.** Most people write
the sentence first because it feels like writing. Do it last. Many viewers never hear it, and
everyone sees the first frame.

**The agreement test.** Each layer has to carry the subject of the promise, and the words a
viewer reads have to share a subject with the words they hear.

- Cover the sound. Do the words on screen and the picture alone tell you what the video is about?
- Cover the screen. Does the spoken line alone tell you the same thing?
- Ask someone what the video is about after two seconds of each. If you get two answers, the
  opening is two videos.

Disagreeing (invented):

```
Promise:   why a loaf comes out dense
On screen: Our Saturday class is back
Show:      Ava waves from the shop doorway.
Say:       A dense loaf is rarely the flour.
```

Three layers, three subjects. The viewer reads about a class, sees a doorway and hears about
flour. Agreeing:

```
Promise:   why a loaf comes out dense, and how to check the proof
On screen: Dense loaf? Check the proof
Show:      Two cut loaves side by side on the bench, one dense and one open.
Say:       A dense loaf is rarely the flour. It is the proof.
```

**Two things every opening needs.**

1. *The right person can tell it is for them.* Use the words in the brand's audience file, not
   trade words. "Dense loaf" is what a home baker says. "Under-fermented crumb" is what a baker says.
2. *Something is left open.* The opening raises a question and the video answers it. If the
   opening already contains the whole answer, there is no reason to stay.

**Two things an opening never does.**

- *It never states a fact the brand cannot back.* A number or a result in the opening must trace
  to the brand's own facts, research or proof. If it does not, open a different way.
- *It never manufactures fear.* Naming a problem the viewer already has is honest. Inventing a
  threat to make them stay is not, and it attracts the wrong people. Promise the outcome.

## 3. Opening patterns

Each pattern below is a complete opening, all three layers together, because the layers are
designed as one thing. For first lines and first pictures as separate lists, see `hooks.md`.

Fill the blanks only with what the brand's files can support. If a pattern needs a number and the
brand has none, pick another pattern.

### The fault on the bench
*Show the failure first. Best for trust.*

```
On screen: [The problem, in their words]? Check [the real cause]
Show:      The failed [thing] next to a good one, same framing.
Say:       A [problem] is rarely [what people blame]. It is [the real cause].
```
Bakery: "Dense loaf? Check the proof" / two cut loaves side by side / "A dense loaf is rarely the
flour. It is the proof."

### The question from the counter
*Open with a question real customers ask, close to word for word. Best for trust.*

```
On screen: "[Their question]"
Show:      The [thing the question is about], in your hands.
Say:       People ask me [their question] most weeks. Here is the honest answer.
```
Clinic: "Should I run through heel pain?" / Tomas pressing a thumb into a runner's heel / "Runners
ask me whether to run through heel pain most weeks. Here is the honest answer."

### The test on camera
*Run a small test whose result is not obvious. Works for reach and trust.*

```
On screen: [Option A] or [option B]? Same [everything else]
Show:      Both set up side by side, the difference visible.
Say:       Same [thing], one change: [the change]. Let us see what it does to [the result].
```
Bakery: "Cold oven or hot? Same dough" / two identical tins, one going into a cold oven / "Same
dough, one change: a cold oven. Let us see what it does to the crust."

### The number from our own bench
*Lead with a number the brand can show. Needs a source.*

```
On screen: [The number] [unit] for [the result]
Show:      The thing the number measures, with the number visible in shot.
Say:       Our [thing] takes [the number] [unit]. Here is what that time is doing.
```
Bakery: "18 hours for one loaf" / the dough tub with yesterday's time on the lid / "Our sourdough
ferments for at least 18 hours. Here is what that time is doing." (Source: the brand's own
production sheet, recorded as a fact.)

### The thing we stopped doing
*A change of mind, told straight. Builds trust quickly.*

```
On screen: Why we stopped [the old way]
Show:      The old way, being done, then set aside.
Say:       I used to [the old way]. I stopped, and [the thing it fixed] is why.
```
Clinic: "Why I stopped giving stretches first" / a printed stretch sheet going into a drawer /
"I used to hand every runner a stretch sheet first. I stopped, and the ones who came back are why."

### Two of the same, one difference
*A comparison the eye reads in a second. Works for every job.*

```
On screen: Same [thing]. One [difference]
Show:      The two versions, identically framed, the difference pointed at.
Say:       These two [things] are the same except for [the difference]. Look at what it changes.
```
Bakery: "Same flour. One hour apart" / two loaves cut open, a clock card beside each / "These two
loaves are the same dough, shaped one hour apart. Look at what it changes."

### The step people skip
*One small step, and what skipping it costs. Best for trust.*

```
On screen: The [small step] most people skip
Show:      The step being done, close up.
Say:       Most [people like the viewer] skip this. It takes [how long], and it decides [the result].
```
Clinic: "The calf check most runners skip" / a single-leg calf raise, close on the heel / "Most
runners skip this check. It takes a minute, and it decides whether the heel pain comes back."

### What right looks like
*Show the finished result first, then how to get there. Best for leads and trust.*

```
On screen: This is [the result] done right
Show:      The finished result, then the first step beginning.
Say:       This is what [the result] looks like when it goes right. It starts with [the first step].
```
Bakery: "This is a proofed dough" / a finger pressing dough, the dent slowly filling / "This is
what a properly proofed dough does when you press it. It starts the night before."

## 4. The body: one idea, said and shown

**One script, one idea.** Every script file opens with `The one idea`: a single sentence. If it
takes two sentences, there are two scripts. Everything after it either serves that sentence or
comes out.

**Every line is a pair.** A line in the script is something said (`Say:`) beside something shown
(`Show:`). There are no spoken lines without a picture. If you cannot think of a picture for a
line, the line is probably abstract. Rewrite it until there is something to point a camera at.

A shape that holds for most scripts (the timings for each platform are in `short-form-video.md`
section 3):

1. **The opening** makes the promise.
2. **Why it matters**, to this viewer, in one line.
3. **The delivery**: two to four lines, one step or one reason each, each with its own picture.
   The first useful thing arrives early. Do not save everything for the end.
4. **Something to do today.** The last line before the ending hands the viewer one action.
5. **The ending**: the one ask that fits the job.

**Write for the mouth, not the page.**

- Short sentences. The brand's own sentence limit applies to every spoken line, and a line over
  it does not pass: a sentence that cannot be said in one breath cannot be recorded.
- Use the words the person actually uses. The brand's voice samples are the reference. A script
  should sound like those samples read aloud.
- No greeting, no "in this video", no recap of what you are about to say. Start on the subject.
- Read it out loud before it is finished. Anything you stumble on gets rewritten, not rehearsed.

**Write pictures an editor could shoot.** A good `Show:` names a thing, an action and a framing:
"Close on a floured finger pressing into shaped dough." A weak one names a mood: "Ava looking
thoughtful." Change the picture with every line. Hands doing something hold the eye better than a
face talking.

**The sourcing law.**

- A spoken line may be refined so it is easy to say. The FACT inside it may not be changed and
  may not be invented.
- Every line that states a fact carries a `Source:` note: where in the brand's own material it
  comes from. Write facts with digits (18 hours, not eighteen) so they can be checked.
- A number that traces to the brand's facts, research or proof passes. One that does not is
  labelled **unverified**, and the person is told. Nothing is ever quietly passed.
- Names on the brand's never-mention list stay out of the pictures as well as the words. A name
  that is filmed is a name that is published.

## 5. The ending: the ask that fits the job

One ask per video. Two asks get neither.

| Job | Allowed endings | Never |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | Save this for [a moment]. Send this to [a kind of person]. Follow for more on [the subject] | An offer or a keyword |
| Trust | A real question the viewer can answer from their own life, or no ask at all | An offer or a keyword |
| Leads | One next step from the brand's own offers, in the brand's own words. Or one keyword, only if its reply is really set up | A second offer. A keyword nobody has set up |

The Factory chooses the ending when a pitch is approved, from the brand's own call-to-action
library, and rotates it across the batch so the same offer is not repeated in every video. A
leads script for a brand with no offer ready gets a soft ending and a note saying what to set up.

**Make the ask follow from the subject.** A video about dense loaves can end on the class that
teaches proofing. It cannot end on the bread subscription without a jolt.

Where to place the ask within the video is a platform matter. See `short-form-video.md` section 4.

## 6. After the video: the path to a next step

The video is the top of a path. If the rest of the path is missing, a good script changes nothing.

**What has to be in place before a leads script is worth filming.**

1. **The profile says who you help, in one sentence.** Most people who want more go there first.
2. **One link, one page, one action.** The page repeats the promise, says exactly what they get,
   and asks for as little as possible.
3. **What was promised arrives straight away.** A keyword that promises "comment STARTER and I
   will send it" is a promise. The Factory will only write a keyword into a script when the
   brand's library marks its reply as really set up.
4. **Someone answers.** A message that waits three days is a lead that went elsewhere.

**A small free thing is the easiest bridge.** Most viewers are not ready to buy. They will often
take something useful that costs nothing: the one-page method, the checklist from the video, the
class dates. Make it the same subject as the video. The closer the match, the more people take it.

*Bakery.* The dense-loaf video ends on the one-page proofing method, the same one class students
take home. *Clinic.* The heel-pain video ends on the calf check written out as a single page.

**When views do not turn into next steps, find where people stop.**

| Where they stop | What it usually means | Try first |
|---|---|---|
| They watch, and do nothing | The ask is vague, or there are two | One ask, named exactly, tied to the subject |
| They visit the profile, and leave | The profile does not say who you help | Rewrite the first line of the profile |
| They reach the page, and do not act | The page asks too much or promises something else | One action. The promise in the same words as the video |
| They take the free thing, and never come back | Nothing follows it | A short series of useful messages, then one offer |
| Plenty of views, none from likely customers | The subjects are too wide for the business | Narrow the subjects. Fewer reach scripts, more trust |

Judge a leads script by next steps for every 1,000 views. A quiet video that fills two class
places did its job better than a loud one that filled none.

## 7. Your first videos, if you are new to a camera

The aim of a first session is to finish, not to be good. Skill comes from finished videos.

**Before.**

- Pick the format you could repeat every week. If the lens frightens you, film your hands and
  talk over them. That format is also one of the strongest for teaching.
- Two or three scripts in one session. Setting up is the slow part. Once it is done, the second
  video costs a fraction of the first.
- Read each teleprompter file out loud once. Where you trip, change the script and check it
  again. Do not train yourself to say a sentence you would never say.
- Gather what the index lists under "to have to hand". Hunting for a prop mid-take ends sessions.

**During.**

- Sound before picture. A quiet room and the phone close to you matter more than the camera.
  Face a window. Buy nothing yet.
- Put the teleprompter as close to the lens as you can, so your eyes stay near it.
- Film one line at a time. Read the line, look at the lens, say it, stop. The teleprompter file
  is already broken into short lines with a gap wherever the picture changes. Every gap is a
  place to breathe, and the cut hides the join.
- Say each line as if to one customer across the counter.
- A take is good enough when the words are clear and the picture shows what the line says. Move on.

**After.**

- Publish all of them before judging any of them. One video tells you nothing.
- Tell Claude which scripts were filmed, so the batch record stays true.
- The first ones will feel awkward to you and ordinary to everyone else. That gap closes on its own.

## 8. Getting better: one change at a time

**Look at batches, not single videos.** One video is noise. Wait until at least four have
numbers, then look at them together. The audit does this for you from your own numbers and never
fills a gap with a guess.

**Four moments, in the order a viewer lives them.** Each one leaves a number behind.

| Moment | The viewer decides | The number it leaves | What in the script moves it |
|---|---|---|---|
| The stop | Do I stop, or swipe past? | Still watching at three seconds | The three layers of the opening, and whether they agree |
| The stay | Do I stay for the point? | Average share of the video watched | One idea, something useful early, a new picture with each line, the length |
| The keep | Is this worth keeping or passing on? | Saves and sends for every 1,000 views | Whether a viewer can do something with it, and whether the facts are shown |
| The step | Do I take the next step? | Next steps for every 1,000 views | The ending: one ask, fitted to the job and the subject |

Read the moments in order. A weak stop hides everything after it: nobody can keep a video they
never watched.

**What is typical, and what stands out.** Typical means the middle value of the batch, because
one runaway video drags an average and barely moves a middle value. A script stands out when it
did about three times typical or more, or a third of typical or less.

**The single-variable rule.** An audit ends in one thing to change. Not three. If the opening,
the length and the ending all change at once and the numbers move, nobody can say which change
did it, and the next batch teaches nothing.

- Change the one thing across the whole next batch.
- Hold everything else still, on purpose, even the parts you dislike.
- Judge the next batch by the same number.
- If two things always changed together in your batch, the numbers cannot separate them. Test
  the one that acts earlier in the video and hold the other still.

**When one script clearly wins, make it again.** Same opening pattern, same format, same length,
same kind of ending. Change only the subject. If the next ones also do well, you have found a
shape worth repeating. If they do not, the first was its subject, or luck.

**What not to conclude.**

- Not "the platform changed". That cannot be tested, so nothing can be learned from it.
- Not "this one flopped, start again". One video is not a pattern.
- Not "views are up, so it is working", when the job was leads.

## 9. How the Factory holds a script to this

The checker reads every script against the brand's own rules before it can be filmed.

| It checks | Why |
|---|---|
| The three layers of the opening agree with the promise and with each other | Section 2 |
| The opening states no fact the brand cannot back | Section 2 |
| One idea, in one sentence | Section 4 |
| Every spoken line has a picture beside it | Section 4 |
| Every spoken sentence is within the brand's limit, with none of its banned words or long dashes | The brand's style |
| No never-mention name is spoken, shown on screen or filmed | The brand's rules |
| Regulated brands: terms that must never publish, and terms that need a disclaimer and a named sign-off | The brand's compliance settings |
| Every fact has a source, and every number traces to the brand's evidence or is labelled unverified | Section 4 |
| The ending fits the job, points only at a real offer, and uses only a keyword that is set up | Section 5 |

A script that passes gets its teleprompter file made in the same step. If the script changes
afterwards, it is a draft again and its teleprompter is marked out of date until it passes again.
