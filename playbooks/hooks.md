# Hooks: first lines and first seconds

> last_researched: 2026-06-11 (as of June 2026)
> A library of opening PATTERNS. Each has a fill-in template and two examples. Every example is
> invented for the two fictional brands in `README.md` (a bakery and a physio clinic). None is a
> real opening line from anyone. The numbers in them are made up to show the pattern.

## How to use this file

1. Look at the contract for the post. What does it actually contain: a real number, a real
   result, a real observation, a photo, a question customers ask?
2. Pick a pattern that material can fill **honestly**.
3. Fill the template. Keep within the platform's visible length.
4. Run the five checks at the end of this file.

**The rule that overrides every pattern:** a template slot is filled only from the contract. If
a pattern needs a number and the brand has no real one, choose another pattern. Never round a
number up, never invent a count, never imply a customer who does not exist.

## What every opening needs

| Ingredient | Meaning | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Something specific | A number, a time span, a named thing | "We improved our process" | "22 loaves wasted a day, down to 3" |
| A gap | The reader wants the next line | "Here are some baking tips" | "The fix cost nothing and hangs on the wall" |
| A signal of who it is for | The right person recognises themselves | "Tips for everyone" | "If your starter is a week old and flat" |

Specific beats clever. Exact numbers read as true and round ones read as made up. The reader's
words beat trade words. An opening that could start anybody's post is not an opening.

# Part 1. Written posts

## LinkedIn
About 140 characters show before "see more". One tight paragraph, no line break inside it.

**1. The result first**
`[Who or what] went from [before] to [after] in [time span]. [A hint at the cause].`
- "Our Tuesday bread waste went from 22 loaves to 3 in six weeks. The fix cost nothing and hangs on the kitchen wall."
- "A runner I work with went from stopping at two miles to finishing a 10K in four months. We changed her week, not her knee."

**2. The belief, corrected**
`Most [people like the reader] believe [common belief]. [What you see instead, in your own work].`
- "Most people think good bread needs a strong arm. The best loaf we sell is barely touched."
- "Most runners believe a sore knee needs rest. In my clinic, the ones who rest longest are often back soonest with the same knee."

**3. "I looked at N of these"**
`I [read / counted / went through] [N] [things] [time span]. [M] of them [shared one surprising thing].`
- "I went through a year of our order book. One loaf outsells the next three combined, and it is not the one on the sign."
- "I looked back at 50 first appointments from this spring. 38 of those runners had raised their mileage in the month before the pain began."

**4. What changed**
`[Something in the reader's world] changed [when]. Here is what it means for [the reader].`
- "The Saturday market moves to the car park from June. Here is where to find us, and what we are baking for the first one."
- "Entries for the autumn races opened this week. If you are starting from zero, these are the 12 weeks that matter."

**5. The list worth keeping**
`The [N] [checks / questions / steps] I use before [a moment the reader also faces]:`
- "The 6 questions I ask before we add a new loaf to the counter:"
- "The 5 things I look at in a runner's training diary before I look at their leg:"

**6. The honest admission**
`I [did something that looks against my own interest] [when]. Here is why.`
- "I talked a customer out of our most expensive class last week. Here is why."
- "I told a runner she did not need to see me again. I should have said it a session earlier."

## Facebook
About 125 characters show before "See more". A concrete noun in every opening: a place, a product, a kind of person.

**1. The benefit promised**
`[A specific good outcome], from [one small, named change].`
- "A crust that stays crisp until dinner, from one change to how you cool the loaf."
- "A two minute routine my runners do before every long run, and why I chose it."

**2. The plain statement of what you will show**
`Here is [exactly what they are about to see], [and the one thing to notice].`
- "Here is one dough baked after 4 hours and after 14, cut open side by side. Look at the holes."
- "Here is what I look at when a runner walks into the clinic, before they have said a word."

**3. The question only they can answer**
`[Named kind of person]: [a question about their own experience]?`
- "Home bakers: what did your very first loaf look like? Ours was a brick."
- "Saturday 5K regulars: what was the week you nearly gave up, and what brought you back?"

**4. The pattern, with a count**
`I have [taught / served / assessed] [N] [people] [time span]. [Nearly all of them] [share one thing].`
- "We have taught 60 beginners to bake this year. Nearly every one made the same mistake with the water."
- "I have assessed more than 200 runners in this clinic. The most common cause of their pain is not the one they expect."

**5. The local line**
`[Place] [people]: [something specific happening here, this week].`
- "Maple Street neighbours: the ovens go on at 4am this Saturday for the summer fair, and the first 50 cinnamon knots are on us."
- "Runners around Riverside Park: path resurfacing starts Monday. Here are three flat loops to use instead."

## Instagram
The opening is the cover slide or the first frame. A headline of 5 to 10 words, readable in under
a second. Caption line 1 (about 125 characters) extends the cover and carries the search phrase.

**1. The specific result**
`[Before] to [after], in [time or count]`
- Cover: "Flat loaf to open crumb in 3 bakes". Caption: "Three sourdough bakes, one change each time. Here is exactly what we changed."
- Cover: "From 2 miles to 10K, same shoes". Caption: "A return to running plan starts with your week, not your kit."

**2. The small mistake with a real cost**
`The [small habit] behind [a cost the reader already feels]`
- Cover: "The fridge habit that flattens your sourdough"
- Cover: "The warm-up shortcut I see before most calf strains"

**3. The unfinished picture**
`[An image with the answer visibly out of frame] + [words that promise the rest]`
- Image: two loaf tins, one under a cloth. Cover text: "Same dough. One of these will fail."
- Image: a training diary with one week circled and the numbers covered. Cover text: "The week that caused it"

**4. The belief reversed**
`[A common instruction], reversed. [What happens instead, promised on the next slide].`
- Cover: "You do not need to knead. Swipe for what we do."
- Cover: "Rest alone rarely fixes a running injury. Here is what I add."

**5. Already under way** (Reel first frame)
`[Hands, body or screen mid-action] + [3 to 7 words naming the payoff]`
- First frame: a knife halfway through a crust, steam rising. Text: "Listen to this crust"
- First frame: a runner mid-stride on a treadmill, filmed from behind. Text: "Watch the left hip"

**6. The reader named**
`If you [a specific situation], [read / save / watch] this`
- Cover: "If your starter is a week old and doing nothing, read this"
- Cover: "If you took up running after 35, save this"

Carousel reminder: slide 2 may be shown first. Write it as a second opening.

## X
On X the opening line has to sell the post by itself. Lead with the result or the reader. No thread label, no link.

**1. The exact number**
`[Thing]: [exact before] to [exact after]. [The cause, in a few words].`
- "Our 7am queue went from 4 people to 19. We moved the oven timer, not the prices."
- "41 of my last 50 runners with shin pain had one thing in common. It was in their diary, not their legs."

**2. The line that frames the image**
`This is [what the image is]. Look at [the one detail].`
- "This is one dough baked in two tins, an hour apart. Look at the top edge." [photo]
- "This is a runner's week before and after we changed it. Look at Thursday." [image]

**3. The belief corrected, evidence next**
`[Common belief] is [wrong / half the story]. Here is what I see instead:`
- "Expensive flour is not what makes bakery bread taste better. Time is. The same recipe at three speeds:"
- "New shoes rarely fix an old injury. Here is what I check first:"

**4. How it works, plainly**
`[Thing] is [its plain parts]. [The consequence most people miss].`
- "A sourdough starter is flour, water and time. The rest is patience."
- "A training plan is stress and rest in the right order. Most injuries I see are an order problem."

**5. The frustration, said back**
`You [did the sensible thing], [it seemed to work], and then [the familiar setback].`
- "You followed the recipe to the gram, the dough looked right, and the loaf still came out flat."
- "You bought the shoes, did the stretches, took a week off, and the same shin still hurts by mile three."

## YouTube titles
Nothing plays until someone clicks, so the title (under about 50 characters) and thumbnail are the first line.

**1. The comparison** `[A] vs [B], [how they are judged]`
- "Packet yeast vs 5 year old starter, same recipe"
- "Cushioned vs flat shoes: what changes in your stride"

**2. The test over time** `I [did X] for [N days]. [What you will see]`
- "I baked the same loaf for 30 days. Loaf 1 vs loaf 30"
- "I filmed my running form weekly for 12 weeks"

**3. The question, as typed** `[The question in the searcher's exact words]?`
- "Why is my sourdough flat?"
- "Why does my knee hurt on stairs after a run?"

**4. The countdown** `[N] [mistakes] [who makes them], and the fix for each`
- "5 shaping mistakes every beginner makes, and the fix for each"
- "4 training diary mistakes I see before most injuries"

# Part 2. Video (the first three seconds)

A video opening has three layers: what is **said**, what is **seen**, and the **text on screen**.
Write them separately. Then make them agree.

## A. Spoken hooks (the first sentence)
Skip the hello and the preamble. The point lands within two to three seconds, in the viewer's own words.

**1. The result first**
`This [thing] [did something notable] [in a time, or under a limit]. Here is [the part you are about to show].`
- "This tray sold out in eleven minutes on Saturday. Here is the one thing we changed on the label."
- "She ran her first 10K in April after two years of stopping at mile two. Here is the week we built."

**2. The belief corrected**
`[Common belief, stated or implied]. [A short contradiction].`
- "You do not need a mixer for this. You need a wet hand and ten seconds."
- "Tight hamstrings are rarely the reason you cannot touch your toes."

**3. The promise with a clock on it**
`In the next [N] seconds I will show you [one specific skill or check].`
- "In the next 20 seconds I will show you how to tell if your dough is ready, using one finger."
- "Give me 30 seconds and I will show you the first thing I check on every runner."

**4. The question that picks the viewer**
`[A situation only the right viewer is in]?`
- "Starter a week old and still flat?"
- "Took up running after 40, and your calves complain every time?"

**5. The live test**
`Let us see what happens if [I break the rule / try the thing]. [The setup, in a few words].`
- "Let us see if a cold oven really ruins a loaf. One goes in hot, one goes in cold."
- "Let us see what a small incline does to your stride. Same runner, same speed."

**6. The number that needs explaining**
`[A real, specific number]. [What it means for the viewer].`
- "78 percent. That is how much of this dough is water, and it is why the loaf stays soft for days."
- "Ten percent. That is the most I let a runner add to their week, and here is why."

## B. Visual hooks (the first frame and the first movement)
The eye decides before the ear. Design the first frame on purpose.

**1. Start mid-action**
`[Hands, body or screen already doing the thing, at its most interesting moment]`
- Dough being slapped onto the bench, flour in the air, from frame one.
- A runner's feet landing on a treadmill in slow motion, already mid-stride.

**2. The object up front**
`[The one object that is the evidence, large in frame]`
- A loaf held cut side to the lens, filling the picture.
- A worn running shoe held sole up, the wear pattern toward the camera.

**3. Two things side by side**
`[Identical framing, two versions, the difference visible at once]`
- Two slices on one board, one dense, one open, labelled "4 hours" and "14 hours".
- Split screen of the same runner in week 1 and week 8, same angle, same speed.

**4. The end state first**
`[The finished result], then a hard cut back to the beginning`
- The full counter at 7am, then a cut to an empty mixing bowl at 4am.
- A runner crossing a finish line, then a cut to her first slow walk on the treadmill.

**5. Text that finishes the picture**
`[3 to 7 words on screen that name what the eye is looking at]`
- Over a loaf sinking in the oven: "Over-proofed. Here is the sign."
- Over a paused frame of a foot landing: "This is the moment that matters"

**6. The drawn mark**
`[An arrow, circle or line drawn live on the picture]`
- A finger presses the dough. A circle is drawn round the dent as it springs back.
- A line is drawn from hip to ankle on a paused frame of the runner.

## C. The agreement rule

**The text on screen, the first spoken line and the first image must make the same promise.**
When they disagree, the viewer has to choose which one to follow, and chooses to leave.

Three tests, in this order:

1. **Sound off.** Watch the first three seconds muted. Do the picture and the text alone carry the promise?
2. **Eyes shut.** Listen to the first sentence. Does it make sense with no picture?
3. **Together.** Does the picture show the evidence for the sentence? It should reinforce, not repeat word for word, and never compete.

| | Spoken | Seen | Text on screen | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agrees | "This is one dough, baked after 4 hours and after 14." | Two cut loaves side by side, a hand pointing at each | "4 hours vs 14 hours" | One promise, three ways |
| Disagrees | "This is one dough, baked after 4 hours and after 14." | The baker talking to camera in the shop | "Our story" | The ear hears a comparison. The eye sees no comparison. The text promises something else |
| Agrees | "Watch her left hip as she lands." | Slow motion from behind, a line drawn at the hip | "Watch the left hip" | One promise, three ways |
| Disagrees | "Watch her left hip as she lands." | A wide shot of the clinic reception | "Running tips" | Nothing on screen to watch |

For long videos the same rule stretches across the thumbnail, the title and the first 30 seconds.

## Openings to retire

- A greeting, your name, your job title, or a logo.
- "So today", "in this video", or any description of what is coming instead of the thing itself.
- Scene-setting that could begin any post on any subject.
- A question whose answer is obviously yes.
- A promise of a shock with the subject hidden. It is demoted as bait and it attracts the wrong people.
- Trade terms the customer would not use.
- Any number, result or customer that is not in the brand's files.
- An opening bigger than the post can pay off.

## Five checks before you keep an opening

1. **Is every fact in it in the contract?**
2. **Would the right reader know it is for them** within the first few words?
3. **Does it fit the visible length?** LinkedIn 140 characters. Facebook and Instagram 125. X: one line. Video: three seconds.
4. **Does the post pay it off,** fully, and soon?
5. **Does it sound like this brand?** Read it next to `Brand/voice-samples.md`. A person says "I". A company says "we" and "you".
