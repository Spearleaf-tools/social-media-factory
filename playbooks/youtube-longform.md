# YouTube long-form rulebook

> last_researched: 2026-06-11 (as of June 2026). Confidence: medium
> Covers videos of roughly 5 to 20 minutes. Shorts are in `short-form-video.md`.
> Basis: YouTube's own help pages, its annual public letter and a published interview with its
> discovery team are solid. Retention and click-through percentages come from analytics vendors
> and creator studies: consistent with each other, not official. Nothing here depends on one
> unverified figure. Every example is invented. See `README.md`.

## 1. How reach works there now

**It pulls, it does not push.** YouTube's discovery team describes the system as finding videos
for each viewer, not sending a video out to an audience. It builds a picture of each person and
fetches what that person is likely to choose and enjoy. So the useful question is never "did the
algorithm like my video?". It is "did a definable group of viewers click it and come away satisfied?"

**The signals, in rough order.**

| Signal | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Viewer satisfaction | Likes and dislikes, survey answers after watching, rewatches, "don't recommend" | YouTube says it cares how viewers feel, not only what they do, and presents this as less easy to manipulate than minutes watched |
| Click-through rate | The way in. Of the people shown the title and thumbnail, the share who click | By YouTube's own help pages about half of channels sit between 2 and 10 percent. 4 to 6 is healthy. Search runs highest, Suggested in the middle, the Home page lowest |
| View duration and share viewed | The multiplier on the click | Vendor benchmarks: average retention is about a quarter, and more than half of viewers leave in the first minute |
| Session contribution | Does the viewer keep watching YouTube afterwards, or leave? | Widely reported to matter for long-form. Not an official ranking order |
| Context | Time of day, device, what was just watched | Related subject matter is what drives Suggested |

**Changes from 2025 and 2026.**

- **Shorts and long-form are recommended separately** (widely reported in late 2025, with no
  single official statement). Do not expect Shorts to lift long videos, or to drag them.
- **Language models read the video.** YouTube uses them to understand what is said and shown.
  Videos whose spoken words clearly state the subject, the steps and the names of things are
  understood better.
- **Built-in title and thumbnail testing.** Up to three thumbnails, and since December 2025 up to
  three titles or title and thumbnail pairs, tested for about two weeks. **The winner is chosen
  by share of watch time, not by click-through.** Desktop Studio only.
- **Where views come from.** The Home page gives a new video its first reach. Suggested compounds
  over months. Search brings the viewers with the most intent, and teaching videos do best there.

**AI assistants quote videos.** Large 2026 studies of what AI answer tools cite found long-form
video among the most quoted kinds of source, with almost all of it long-form and the biggest
share between 10 and 20 minutes. View counts, likes and subscriber numbers had close to no
relationship with being quoted. What did: clear structure, a full description, and recency.
A well-organised video from a small channel can be quoted ahead of a popular, rambling one.

## 2. First lines that earn the click and the watch

On long-form the opening is a gate with three stages.

### Stage 0. The title and thumbnail
Nothing plays automatically, so the packaging does the job a first frame does elsewhere.
Successful channels put a large share of their effort into the idea, the title and the thumbnail
before anything is filmed. Small channels usually put in almost none.

- **Titles:** under about 50 characters. Concrete. A single promise. Dependable frames: a comparison
  ("Shop loaf vs 14 hour sourdough, cut open"), a before and after, a test over time
  ("I fed my starter rye flour for 30 days. Here is what changed"), a plain question.
- **Thumbnails:** understood in a one second glance. Three focal points at most. Four or five
  words of text at most. A plain, bold image does better than a carefully designed one.
- **A real picture out-clicks stock.** The cut loaf, the real chart, the actual foot on the actual treadmill.
- **Always test.** Two or three variants, one element changed.

### Stage 1. The first 5 to 30 seconds
Holding 70 percent of viewers at the 30 second mark is strong. Introductions longer than about 20
seconds usually fail. The shape is well replicated even where the exact numbers are not.

**Spoken opening patterns** (templates in `hooks.md`):

| Pattern | Invented example |
|---|---|
| The result first | "This loaf cost 61 pence to make and I would put it against any in the city. Every step is coming up." |
| A claim that needs proving | "Most advice about shin splints makes them last longer. Let me show you why on a real runner." |
| A question that involves the viewer | "Is your starter really active, or does it only look it? Let us test mine, now." |
| The cost of a small thing | "One habit in the first mile is behind most of the calf strains I treat." |

**Visual opening.**

- Open on the evidence: the most interesting moment first, then go back and explain how you got there.
- Movement and annotation: zoom in, draw the circle live, point at the thing.
- Every spoken claim gets its picture within about three seconds. Say "twice the rise" and the two loaves are on screen.

**Openings to drop:** your name, a channel introduction, a description of the video's contents, a logo
animation, or a request to subscribe.

**Agreement.** Thumbnail, spoken claim and on-screen evidence all promise one thing. Within 30
seconds the viewer should be looking at what the thumbnail showed them. A mismatch is
what viewers call clickbait, and watch-time-based testing plus satisfaction surveys punish it.

### Stage 2. From 0:30 to 2:00
1. One line of credibility **through showing, not biography** ("I teach this to 12 beginners every Saturday. These three loaves are from last week's class").
2. What is coming: how many parts there are, and which one is worth waiting for.
3. Deliver the first item straight away.

About half of viewers are gone within the first minute, so nothing in this stretch can be filler.

## 3. Post skeletons

Under all of them: a change of some kind every 30 to 45 seconds (new visual, new number, a direct
question, a change of pace). Each section has its own setup, development and payoff. Plant one or
two open questions early and answer them late. Win the viewer back around the halfway mark.
(This is craft guidance from people who teach video making. It is sound practice, not platform law.)

### A. The Worked Example (10 to 15 minutes)
*Use it when you have a real case from start to finish and can show every step.*

Timings are given as a share of the running time, so the skeleton stretches to any length.

```
Opening 20 seconds   The outcome, on screen, before any explanation. Real figures and the time it took.
To the 1 minute mark Why this case matters to the viewer, and what they will be shown, in order.
Next 10 percent      The starting state, looked at closely. Tell the viewer that one fault did more
                     harm than all the others, and keep back which one.
Middle 55 percent    Three to five chapters, one change each. For every change: show it being made,
                     explain it with one everyday comparison, then show what it altered.
                     Shift something at every chapter break: the shot, the pace, the voice.
At the halfway point Win them back. Say what the coming chapter settles and why it outweighs the last.
Next 15 percent      Name the fault you held back. Play the whole timeline from start to finish.
Final minute         Who this works for and who it does not. One ask. End screen: the nearest related video.
```

### B. The Live Test (8 to 12 minutes)
*Use it when seeing is believing, and the outcome is not known in advance.*

```
Opening 25 seconds   Ask the question while the test is already under way on screen.
To the 1 minute mark The method: what is being tried, and which outcome would mean what.
Middle 60 percent    Run it. One chapter per scenario. Keep it feeling unedited, and zoom in when
                     something surprises you. A scoreboard stays on screen and is updated each
                     round, which gives viewers something to come back to.
At the halfway point Win them back. Say what the next round is going to decide.
Next 20 percent      The meaning for the viewer: two or three actions, one everyday comparison for each idea.
Final minute         One ask. End screen: a related test.
```

### C. The Countdown of Mistakes (8 to 12 minutes)
*Use it for reach inside your subject, when you have seen the same errors many times.*

```
Opening 20 seconds   The pattern and its count: "I have watched 60 beginners shape a loaf. Five things go wrong, again and again."
Next 25 seconds      What is coming, and a hint at number one.
Mistakes 5 down to 1 A chapter each. Show it happening, say what it costs, give the one minute repair,
                     show the repaired result. The countdown itself keeps a question open.
At mistake three     Win them back before the top two.
After number one     Which single repair to make first, and the reason.
Final minute         One ask (a checklist, a class, a booking). End screen.
```

### D. The Straight Answer (5 to 8 minutes)
*Use it for a question people type into search. It is also the format AI answer tools quote most readily.*

```
0:00-0:15   Say the question aloud, in the words people use. Promise the short answer, then the full one.
0:15-1:00   The short answer, complete, in 30 to 60 seconds.
1:00-5:00   The full answer in two to four chapters: why, how, exceptions.
5:00-end    One common mistake, one ask, end screen.
```

**Chapters.** Add timestamps to every video. People who skip stay, and clearly labelled segments
are what search and AI tools link into. Name chapters as questions or claims ("Why your starter
smells of nail varnish"), never "Part 2".

**How this differs from short video.** Short video needs a payoff every few seconds and is over
inside a minute. Long video may take its time, yet it has to win the viewer back roughly twice a minute
and must answer every question it plants. An unanswered question damages satisfaction. The
opening logic is identical: a claim, its evidence on screen, and one open question.

## 4. Length, links, hashtags and calls to action

**Length.** The general range in 2025 and 2026 is 7 to 15 minutes. A sensible target is 8 to 14.
**Let retention set the length. Do not aim at a number of minutes.** Cut whatever does not hold
attention, however short the video becomes.
How-to videos hold viewers better than any other category, because the viewer came to learn.

**Calls to action.**

- **Deliver before you ask for a subscription.** That request belongs once the video has kept its
  main promise, or in the closing moments.
- **A single business ask per video.** Say it midway, straight after you have delivered something, and again as you finish.
- **The spoken close is "watch this next", not "subscribe".** It extends the session, which is a signal.
- **Cards** between 50 and 75 percent of the way through, never during the opening.
- **End screen** over the final 5 to 20 seconds, two elements at most: one "watch next" video and
  subscribe. Keep talking over it.

**Links.** In the first two lines of the description, where they show without expanding.

**Description.** Write a real one. Open with the question being answered, give the answer in
brief, then list the chapters. Description length was the strongest metadata factor in the AI citation
studies, and it helps search.

**Hashtags.** Use three to five that fit, placed in the description. YouTube displays the first
three above the title. More than 60 and the platform ignores them all. They are a minor sorting signal and
cannot rescue a weak title or weak retention.

**Say the names of things out loud.** The transcript feeds both YouTube's understanding of the
video and what AI tools can lift from it.

## 5. Formats that work

- **Screen or demonstration plus face, mixed.** Face for trust, the demonstration for proof. Slides
  with a voice-over do worse on trust. A person talking with nothing shown does worse on proof.
- **Shorts on their own track.** Treat them as a funnel of their own, and link each one to the long video.
- **A mix of three kinds:** answers to searched questions (evergreen), tests and breakdowns that win
  on title and thumbnail (reach), and worked examples with real results (for people close to buying).
- **About four in five videos in a proven skeleton,** one in five an experiment.
- **Give a video a quarter or two before judging it.** Suggested and Search build slowly.

## 6. What to avoid

1. **Reach from the wrong people.** A broad, baited title pulls viewers who leave early and rate
   the video poorly. The system then finds more people like them for your next video.
2. **A thumbnail the video does not deliver.** Make the picture bold if you like. Keep the claim exactly true.
3. **The branded introduction and the biography opening.** Earn trust with what is on screen in the first minute.
4. **Drifting into opinion to camera.** If there is nothing to show, it is a Short or a written post.
5. **Padding to reach a length.**
6. **Short-video habits in long video.** No frantic pacing, no walls of captions, no hashtag piles.
7. **Showing customer details without permission.** Blur or remove them unless you have clear
   permission. An anonymous real case is still better than an invented one.

Decisions to make per brand:

- **Title in the customer's words.** "Why is my sourdough flat?" beats "Optimising fermentation schedules".
- **Which skeleton first?** With real cases, start at A. With none yet, start at D and B.
- **One comparison per idea, one idea per chapter.** Plain comparisons are what viewers repeat.
- **Reply to every real comment.** Comments feed satisfaction, and commenters are warm leads.
- **Regulated brand?** Demonstrate and explain. No outcome promises. Include the brand's standing
  disclaimer in the video and the description.
